## Project showcase

### Growth at the top, erosion at the bottom: U.S. family income, 1972–2016

**Research question.** Between 1972 and 2016, did American family incomes grow, stagnate, or spread
apart — and how much of the change is accounted for by education?

**Why it matters.** The headline income series is usually quoted as one number, the median. A median
hides the shape around it. If the bottom falls while the top stays flat, a "stagnant median" is not
the absence of change; it is a redistribution.

**Data.** General Social Survey (GSS), 1972–2016 — 55,343 U.S. respondents, survey-weighted, with
income in constant dollars (a 1972 dollar and a 2016 dollar are the same unit).

![Cumulative distribution of U.S. family income, 1970s versus 2010s](figures/fig1_income_cdf_1970s_vs_2010s.png)

**What the figure shows.** Each curve is the cumulative distribution of real family income for one
period, drawn on a log axis so that proportional gaps are read as equal distances. The 2010s curve
(the darker line) sits to the *left* of the 1970s curve for the bottom half of the population — at
the 10th percentile real income is 26.5% lower — and the two curves converge near the 90th percentile,
where the change is under 2%. The flattening above the 90th percentile is not a finding about the
rich: it is where the survey stops measuring, because income is top-coded.

**Key findings.**

*The distribution did not shift — it spread: the bottom lost about a quarter of its real income, the
top barely moved, and education is the line that separates the two.*

* The bottom lost ground: p10 fell 26.5%, p25 fell 22.3%, and the median fell 11.5% in constant dollars.
* The top held rather than gained: p90 moved +1.2% over four decades.
* The gap widened: the p75/p25 ratio rose from 2.58 to 3.51 and the Gini from 0.33 to 0.38.
* Education separates the trends: median income fell 33.8% for respondents without a high-school
  diploma and rose 12.3% for graduates; the bachelor's premium widened from 1.34 to 1.82.

**One limitation.** GSS income is top-coded, and the cap absorbs 7–11% of respondents per period.
Every inequality measure in the project is therefore a *lower bound*: the direction of the result is
robust, the magnitude is not.
